# Progress Monitoring, Progress Visibility, and Motivation in Goal Pursuit

Scope note: all figures below are tagged as either **primary-verified** (taken from the
publisher's own abstract/record) or **secondary** (only found via aggregator, blog, or
search-summary). Do not quote secondary numbers in a case study without one more check
at the source.

## Q1. Meta-analytic evidence: does monitoring goal progress promote goal attainment?

### Takeaway
The single strongest citation available is Harkin et al. (2016), *Psychological Bulletin*:
138 randomized studies, N = 19,951, with a small-to-medium but highly reliable effect of
progress monitoring on goal attainment (d+ = 0.40), and — critically for a product that
displays progress — the effect was **larger when progress was reported/made public and when
it was physically recorded**. That moderator is the direct empirical warrant for "make
progress visible," not merely "track progress."

### Cited Findings
- Full citation: Harkin, B., Webb, T. L., Chang, B. P. I., Prestwich, A., Conner, M., Kellar, I., Benn, Y., & Sheeran, P. (2016). "Does monitoring goal progress promote goal attainment? A meta-analysis of the experimental evidence." *Psychological Bulletin*, 142(2), 198–229. — [PubMed record](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26479070/) **(primary-verified)**
- "A systematic literature search identified 138 studies (N = 19,951) that randomly allocated participants to an intervention designed to promote monitoring of goal progress versus a control condition." — [PubMed abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26479070/) **(primary-verified)**
- Interventions increased the frequency of progress monitoring with a very large effect: **d+ = 1.98, 95% CI [1.71, 2.24]** — [PubMed abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26479070/) **(primary-verified)**
- Interventions promoted goal attainment: **d+ = 0.40, 95% CI [0.32, 0.48]** (random-effects model) — [PubMed abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26479070/) **(primary-verified)**
- Mediation: "changes in the frequency of progress monitoring mediated the effect of the interventions on goal attainment" — i.e. the monitoring itself, not some incidental feature of the interventions, carried the effect. — [PubMed abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26479070/) **(primary-verified)**
- The headline moderator, quotable nearly verbatim: "progress monitoring had larger effects on goal attainment when the outcomes were reported or made public, and when the information was physically recorded." — [PubMed abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26479070/) **(primary-verified)**
- All studies in the pool were required to report effects on *both* monitoring frequency and goal attainment, which is an unusually strict inclusion rule and strengthens the causal reading. — [PubMed abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26479070/) **(primary-verified)**
- APA's own press summary of the paper is hosted at [apa.org/pubs/journals/releases/bul-bul0000025.pdf](https://www.apa.org/pubs/journals/releases/bul-bul0000025.pdf); an open-access copy of the accepted manuscript is deposited at [White Rose Research Online](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/87431/). Both are good "verify at source" links for a case study's footnotes.

### Inferences
- d+ = 0.40 is conventionally "small-to-medium." For a designer, the honest framing is not "progress bars transform behavior" but "a reliable, replicated, medium-sized lift across ~20k participants and many domains" — which is unusually strong for a single UI affordance.
- The public-reporting/physical-recording moderator maps almost exactly onto the design choice of surfacing progress in a persistent, visible UI artifact (a bar, a checklist, a percentage) rather than leaving it implicit in the user's head. This is the most directly citable sentence in the entire literature for this case study.

### Gaps
- I could not extract the *numeric* moderator effect sizes (i.e. the separate d+ values for public vs. private, recorded vs. not-recorded subgroups) — those live in the full text tables, and both the APA PDF and the Columbia PDF failed text extraction in this environment. The abstract only states the direction. **Flag this as needing a full-text check before quoting a number.**
- Heterogeneity statistics (I², Q) and publication-bias analyses were not retrievable from the abstract.

## Q2. The goal-gradient effect and illusory / endowed progress

### Takeaway
Two distinct 2006 papers are routinely conflated in design writing: **Kivetz, Urminsky & Zheng
(2006, JMR)** for the goal-gradient + illusionary-progress café-card work, and **Nunes & Drèze
(2006, JCR)** for the car-wash "endowed progress" study. The car wash study is **not** Kivetz —
getting this attribution right matters for a portfolio piece.

### Cited Findings
- Kivetz, R., Urminsky, O., & Zheng, Y. (2006). "The Goal-Gradient Hypothesis Resurrected: Purchase Acceleration, Illusionary Goal Progress, and Customer Retention." *Journal of Marketing Research*, 43(1), 39–58. — [SAGE/JMR record](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1509/jmkr.43.1.39); author's PDF at [home.uchicago.edu/ourminsky](https://home.uchicago.edu/ourminsky/Goal-Gradient_Illusionary_Goal_Progress.pdf) **(citation primary-verified)**
- The goal-gradient hypothesis is the classic behaviorist finding "that animals expend more effort as they approach a reward"; the authors extend it to human reward psychology using field experiments, secondary customer data, and paper-and-pencil problems, with Tobit and logit models. — [JMR abstract](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1509/jmkr.43.1.39) **(primary-verified)**
- Core result: "providing an illusion of goal progress accelerates the rate of goal achievement (i.e., reduced intervisit times for a rewards program) and increases retention in the rewards program." — [JMR abstract](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1509/jmkr.43.1.39) **(primary-verified)**
- Café field experiment: in a "buy 10 coffees, get 1 free" program, the time between purchases dropped by roughly **20%** as customers approached the reward. — reported in search-summary of the paper and in [Columbia Business School's own writeup](https://business.columbia.edu/insights/chazen-global-insights/goal-gradient-hypothesis-resurrected-purchase-acceleration) **(SECONDARY — verify the exact % and the model coefficient in the JMR full text before quoting)**
- Illusionary progress condition: customers given a **12-stamp card with 2 "bonus" stamps pre-applied** completed their 10 required purchases faster than customers given a plain **10-stamp card** — median completion reported as **10 vs 15 days**. Both cards required exactly 10 purchases. — **(SECONDARY; surfaced in search summaries, not confirmed at source in this pass)**
- Nunes, J. C., & Drèze, X. (2006). "The Endowed Progress Effect: How Artificial Advancement Increases Effort." *Journal of Consumer Research*, 32(4), 504–512. — [SSRN record](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=991962) **(citation primary-verified)**
- Car wash field experiment: customers given a **10-space card with 2 spaces pre-stamped** completed the card at **34%**, versus **19%** for customers given an **8-space empty card** — both requiring exactly 8 purchases, same reward. Roughly a **1.8x** lift from framing alone. — [Nunes & Drèze SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=991962); figures repeated in [Learning Loop](https://learningloop.io/plays/psychology/endowed-progress-effect) and [Silicon Canals](https://siliconcanals.com/t-car-wash-loyalty-cards-endowed-progress/) **(the 34%/19% pair is widely and consistently reported, but I only saw it in secondary sources this pass — the ~300-customer N is secondary only)**
- Definition, quotable: endowing a consumer "with some measure of artificial progress toward a given goal can subsequently increase the consumer's motivation to complete the goal," yielding faster and higher goal attainment. — [Nunes & Drèze abstract via SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=991962) **(primary-verified)**

### Inferences
- The endowed-progress and illusionary-progress results together establish something stronger than "progress motivates": **perceived** remaining distance, not actual remaining work, governs effort. Two designs with identical real workloads produce materially different completion rates purely from how progress is framed.
- For a task app, the design implication is that a goal shown as "3 of 12 done" is not informationally equivalent to "9 remaining," and the framing choice is a real lever — see Q6 (small-area hypothesis) for which framing wins when.

### Gaps
- Kivetz et al.'s "post-reward reset" finding (effort dropping back after the reward is claimed) and the Internet-ratings study numbers could not be extracted — the two hosted PDFs (Chicago and Columbia mirrors) returned unparseable binary in this environment.
- Exact N for the café study and for the car wash study remain unverified at source.

## Q3. Amabile & Kramer's Progress Principle

### Takeaway
The single best-known evidence that *visible everyday progress* drives motivation in knowledge
work. The methodology (a large diary study of real project teams, not a lab task) is what makes
it quotable for a productivity product. The headline: of everything that can lift emotion,
motivation, and perception during a workday, **making progress in meaningful work is the single
most important** — and managers systematically fail to recognize this.

### Cited Findings
- Amabile, T. M., & Kramer, S. J. (2011). "The Power of Small Wins." *Harvard Business Review*, May 2011 — [hbr.org/2011/05/the-power-of-small-wins](https://hbr.org/2011/05/the-power-of-small-wins). Book: *The Progress Principle: Using Small Wins to Ignite Joy, Engagement, and Creativity at Work* (Harvard Business Review Press, 2011), ISBN 9781422198575. **(citations primary-verified)**
- Method and sample: approximately **12,000 daily diary entries** from **238 professionals** across **26 project teams** in **7 companies**. Participants rated their "inner work life" (emotions, perceptions, motivations) daily and described one standout event from the day. — [Creativity at Work summary](https://www.creativityatwork.com/on-the-power-of-small-wins/) and [HBR article page](https://hbr.org/2011/05/the-power-of-small-wins) **(the 12,000 / 238 / 7-companies figures are consistently reported across sources; the "26 project teams" figure is secondary)**
- The progress principle, quotable: of all the things that can boost emotions, motivation, and perceptions during a workday, **the single most important is making progress in meaningful work**. — [HBR](https://hbr.org/2011/05/the-power-of-small-wins) **(primary-verified as the article's thesis)**
- Best-day / worst-day asymmetry: **steps forward occurred on 76% of people's best-mood days**, while **setbacks occurred on only 13%** of those days; on the worst days, the most common trigger was setbacks — being blocked or unable to move forward. — reported via [a PDF copy of the HBR article](https://www.develor.si/wp-content/uploads/2022/05/The-power-of-small-wins.pdf) and [mindsetonline summary](https://mindsetonline.com/teresa-amabile-progress-principle-12000-diary-entries-motivator/) **(SECONDARY — high confidence, widely repeated, but the 76%/13% pair should be confirmed against the HBR full text)**
- The "small wins" point: a substantial subset of events participants rated as having only **minor** significance to the project (reported as ~**28%** of events) still had a **major** effect on how they felt about their work that day. — **(SECONDARY, single-source in this pass; treat the 28% as soft until confirmed)**
- The manager-blindness stat: in a survey of **669 managers** across multiple companies and countries, only **5% (35 of 669)** ranked "supporting progress" as the top motivator — managers ranked it **dead last** among five options (behind recognition, incentives, interpersonal support, and clear goals). — [Creativity at Work](https://www.creativityatwork.com/on-the-power-of-small-wins/) confirms the 5% figure; the 669 N and the last-place ranking come from [secondary summaries](https://mindsetonline.com/teresa-amabile-progress-principle-12000-diary-entries-motivator/) **(mixed; 5% is reasonably solid, 35/669 is secondary)**

### Inferences
- The diary methodology is a real strength for a product case study: it is longitudinal, in-situ, and about knowledge work specifically, which is closer to a task-management product's context than any lab study.
- The manager-blindness finding is a rhetorically useful framing for a product argument: progress is the most potent motivator *and* the most systematically under-served one, which is exactly the gap a product can claim to fill.

### Gaps
- HBR's article page is paywalled/truncated to fetch, so the best percentages (76%/13%, 28%, 669) could only be sourced secondarily this pass. **This is the weakest sourcing in the whole note and should be shored up by reading the HBR PDF or the book directly.**
- I found no reported effect sizes or inferential statistics for the Progress Principle work — it is largely descriptive/frequency-based diary analysis, not an experiment. Be careful not to present it as causal evidence.

## Q4. Goal decomposition, subgoals, implementation intentions, and the planning fallacy

### Takeaway
The strongest quantified support for "break the goal down and specify the steps" is
**Gollwitzer & Sheeran (2006)**: 94 independent tests, >8,000 participants, **d = 0.65** for
implementation intentions on goal attainment — a medium-to-large effect, notably larger than
Harkin's monitoring effect. Separately, **the most-cited paper on self-imposed deadlines
(Ariely & Wertenbroch 2002) was retracted in September 2026 for data tampering** — a designer
citing it today would be citing a retracted paper.

### Cited Findings
- Gollwitzer, P. M., & Sheeran, P. (2006). "Implementation Intentions and Goal Achievement: A Meta-Analysis of Effects and Processes." *Advances in Experimental Social Psychology*, 38, 69–119. — [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/chapter/bookseries/abs/pii/S0065260106380021); [author's institutional page](https://www.socmot.uni-konstanz.de/publications/implementation-intentions-and-goal-achievement-meta-analysis-effects-and-processes) **(citation primary-verified)**
- "Findings from 94 independent tests showed that implementation intentions had a positive effect of medium-to-large magnitude (**d = .65**) on goal attainment," across **more than 8,000 participants**. — [Konstanz record](https://www.socmot.uni-konstanz.de/publications/implementation-intentions-and-goal-achievement-meta-analysis-effects-and-processes) **(primary-verified against the published abstract)**
- The mechanism is the if-then specification: "If situation Y is encountered, then I will initiate goal-directed behavior X!" Implementation intentions promoted (a) initiation of goal striving, (b) shielding ongoing pursuit from unwanted influences, (c) disengagement from failing courses of action, and (d) conservation of capability for future striving. — [Konstanz record](https://www.socmot.uni-konstanz.de/publications/implementation-intentions-and-goal-achievement-meta-analysis-effects-and-processes) **(primary-verified)**
- A later meta-analysis of **mental contrasting with implementation intentions (MCII)** on goal attainment is open-access at [PMC8149892](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8149892/) — useful if the case study wants a post-2020 citation for the same construct. **(not read in detail this pass)**
- **RETRACTION (important):** Ariely, D., & Wertenbroch, K. (2002). "Procrastination, Deadlines, and Performance: Self-Control by Precommitment." *Psychological Science*, 13(3), 219–224 — the canonical citation for "evenly spaced intermediate deadlines beat one final deadline" — **was retracted on 2 September 2026**. — [Retraction notice, Psychological Science](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/full/10.1177/09567976261488042); [Retraction Watch coverage](https://retractionwatch.com/2026/09/03/procrastination-study-duke-dan-ariely-psychological-science-data-colada-tampering-retraction/) **(primary-verified)**
- Retraction details: coauthor Klaus Wertenbroch requested the retraction on **23 July 2026**; the notice cited a replication study that "raised questions about the underlying data in the original study" and Data Colada posts (31 Aug / 2 Sep 2026) that "called into question the veracity of the overall findings." The paper had been cited **nearly 1,000 times** per Web of Science. — [Retraction Watch](https://retractionwatch.com/2026/09/03/procrastination-study-duke-dan-ariely-psychological-science-data-colada-tampering-retraction/) **(primary-verified)**
- The failed replication: Hyndman, K., & Bisin, A. (2026). "Replication of 'Procrastination, Deadlines, and Performance: Self-Control by Precommitment.'" *Psychological Science* — found **evenly spaced deadlines had a negligible effect on performance** with adult participants; results did not replicate. — [SAGE](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/09567976261460772) **(primary-verified)**

### Inferences
- The cleanest defensible claim for decomposition in a product case study is via implementation intentions (d = 0.65, 94 tests), not via the deadline-spacing literature, which has just lost its flagship result.
- Structuring a vague goal into concrete, scheduled subtasks is functionally an implementation-intention generator: it converts "finish the report" into "at 9am Tuesday I will draft section 2." That framing lets a product borrow the strongest effect size in this note.
- Honest caveat worth stating in the case study: d = 0.65 comes from self-formed if-then plans in study contexts; whether an app auto-generating subtasks produces the same effect as a user forming them is genuinely untested.

### Gaps
- I did not locate a dedicated meta-analysis of **subgoal specificity or task partitioning** per se (distinct from implementation intentions). Soman & Shi's work on partitioned resources and Amir & Ariely's subgoal work were not verified this pass. **If the report needs a decomposition-specific effect size, it is not in this note.**
- **Planning fallacy:** I did not retrieve Kahneman & Tversky (1979) / Buehler, Griffin & Ross (1994) figures this pass. The commonly cited Buehler et al. finding (students finishing theses far later than their own "realistic" and even "worst-case" estimates) is real but unverified here — do not quote a number for it from this note.

## Q5. Progress bars and progress psychology in software/UX

### Takeaway
**This is the weakest-evidenced section.** The design-community claims about progress bars
overwhelmingly trace back to the *consumer/loyalty-card* studies in Q2 rather than to
software-specific experiments; the primary-source HCI literature was not located in this pass.

### Cited Findings
- The endowed-progress effect is directly and explicitly applied to UX (onboarding checklists starting at non-zero, pre-filled profile-completion bars, game quest logs) — [Learning Loop play](https://learningloop.io/plays/psychology/endowed-progress-effect), [Psychology of Games on quest design](https://www.psychologyofgames.com/2010/11/endowed-progress-effect-and-game-quests/) **(SECONDARY — these are practitioner writeups extrapolating from Nunes & Drèze, not independent software experiments)**
- One peer-reviewed software-adjacent study surfaced: Kundisch, D., & von Rechenberg, T., "Does the Framing of Progress Towards Virtual Rewards Matter? Empirical Evidence..." *Business & Information Systems Engineering*, 59(4) — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s12599-016-0441-5) / [AIS eLibrary](https://aisel.aisnet.org/bise/vol59/iss4/2/). This tests to-date vs to-go progress framing in an online/gamified setting. **(citation located but full text not read this pass)**
- A related recent effect: "The trivial-task motivation effect: highlighting completion of an initial trivial task increases motivation for the main task," *Marketing Letters* (2023) — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11002-023-09692-5) **(citation located, content not verified)**

### Inferences
- A portfolio case study is on safe ground saying "progress-bar design draws on the endowed-progress and goal-gradient findings from consumer psychology," and on shaky ground implying there is a large body of *software-specific* experimental work proving progress bars increase task completion.

### Gaps
- I found **no** verified primary HCI source (e.g. Myers 1985 on progress indicators, or modern A/B-tested onboarding-checklist studies) in this pass. **Flag any progress-bar-specific claim in the final report as practitioner inference, not research finding.**
- No verified quantified conversion/completion lift from a real product's progress-bar A/B test.

## Q6. Counter-evidence and nuance: when progress feedback backfires

### Takeaway
There are two well-established, peer-reviewed limits: **licensing** (perceived progress on a
goal frees people to act against it) and the **small-area hypothesis** (whether to show
completed or remaining progress *reverses* depending on how far along the person is). A case
study that names these reads as far more credible than one that only cites Harkin.

### Cited Findings
- Fishbach, A., & Dhar, R. (2005). "Goals as Excuses or Guides: The Liberating Effect of Perceived Goal Progress on Choice." *Journal of Consumer Research*, 32(3), 370–377. — [Oxford Academic](https://academic.oup.com/jcr/article-abstract/32/3/370/1867208) **(citation primary-verified)**
- Four studies "test whether perceived goal progress hinders the pursuit of the focal goal," demonstrating that "progress along one goal liberates people to pursue inconsistent goals" — i.e. **making progress toward a goal can result in disengagement from that goal**. Merely *planning* future progress can also license incongruent immediate action. — [JCR abstract](https://academic.oup.com/jcr/article-abstract/32/3/370/1867208) **(primary-verified)**
- Koo, M., & Fishbach, A. (2012). "The Small-Area Hypothesis: Effects of Progress Monitoring on Goal Adherence." *Journal of Consumer Research*, 39(3), 493–509. — [Oxford Academic](https://academic.oup.com/jcr/article-abstract/39/3/493/1822606) **(citation primary-verified)**
- The hypothesis, quotable: individuals striving toward a goal "exhibit greater motivation when their attention is directed to whichever is **smaller in size** — their accumulated or remaining progress." — [JCR abstract](https://academic.oup.com/jcr/article-abstract/39/3/493/1822606) **(primary-verified)**
- Practical reversal: early in pursuit, showing **accumulated** progress beats showing remaining ("20% completed" > "80% remaining"); late in pursuit this flips and **remaining** progress is the stronger motivator. The mechanism is an illusion of fast progress created by focusing on the small quantity. — [JCR abstract](https://academic.oup.com/jcr/article-abstract/39/3/493/1822606) **(primary-verified)**
- Koo, M., & Fishbach, A. (2008). "Dynamics of self-regulation: How (un)accomplished goal actions affect motivation." *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*, 94(2), 183–195 — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18211171/). The underlying commitment account: **to-date** information signals commitment and boosts motivation when commitment is uncertain; **to-go** information signals lack of progress and boosts motivation when commitment is already certain. Four studies (exam studying, luxury consumption, desire fulfillment, first-time charitable contributions). — **(primary-verified)**
- Nuance on the goal-gradient itself: Kivetz et al.'s acceleration effect is measured toward a *reward*; motivation is known to reset after the reward is claimed (the "post-reward reset"), meaning a completed progress bar can produce a motivational trough. — **(the reset is described in [practitioner writeups](https://productphilosophy.com/articles/goal-gradient-progress-mechanics); I could NOT verify the original Kivetz numbers for it — treat as unverified)**
- Methodological nuance worth carrying: the retraction of Ariely & Wertenbroch (2002) and its failed 2026 replication (see Q4) is a live reminder that heavily-cited motivation findings in this space are not all safe. — [Retraction Watch](https://retractionwatch.com/2026/09/03/procrastination-study-duke-dan-ariely-psychological-science-data-colada-tampering-retraction/) **(primary-verified)**

### Inferences
- Licensing + small-area together imply a static, always-identical progress display is provably suboptimal: the same "60% done" readout that motivates an uncommitted beginner can license a committed user to coast.
- The genuinely honest version of the product claim is narrower than "progress visibility increases motivation": it is "*well-framed, recorded, externally-visible* progress increases goal attainment by a reliable medium-sized margin, and the correct framing changes over the life of the goal."

### Gaps
- No effect sizes retrieved for Fishbach & Dhar (2005) or Koo & Fishbach (2012) — both abstracts are directional only, and both full texts are paywalled (JCR/Oxford returned 403).
- I found **no meta-analysis of the licensing effect** in this pass, so its robustness relative to Harkin's monitoring effect is unknown. Given that adjacent literatures (ego depletion, moral licensing) have had notable replication problems, this is worth caveating explicitly rather than presenting licensing as settled.
- No source located on whether progress feedback specifically demotivates in *software task-management* contexts, as opposed to consumption/health contexts.
