# The Problem: Long-Horizon Knowledge Work Lacks Visible Progress Signal

Scope note: this file compiles *quantified, attributable* evidence that (a) procrastination is widespread, (b) ambiguous/long-horizon tasks are procrastinated on disproportionately, (c) long-horizon intellectual projects are abandoned at high rates, (d) to-do lists fail as a progress instrument, and (e) visible progress is the single strongest driver of motivation in knowledge work. Every figure is dated. Peer-reviewed findings are labeled; industry/vendor figures are flagged as such; weakly-sourced viral statistics are flagged explicitly.

Research date: 2026-09-20.

## Documented rates of procrastination among students and knowledge workers

### Takeaway
The canonical peer-reviewed anchor is Piers Steel's *Psychological Bulletin* (2007) meta-analysis: roughly 20% of adults self-identify as chronic procrastinators, and 80–95% of college students procrastinate to some degree, with ~50% doing so consistently and problematically. Workplace figures exist but are weaker — the best-cited is that roughly a quarter of the average working day is spent procrastinating (D'Abate & Eddy, 2007).

### Cited Findings
- Steel, P. (2007), "The Nature of Procrastination: A Meta-Analytic and Theoretical Review of Quintessential Self-Regulatory Failure," *Psychological Bulletin* 133(1), 65–94 — the field's standard meta-analysis, based on 691 correlations. Defines procrastination as "to voluntarily delay an intended course of action despite expecting to be worse off for the delay." — [PubMed 17201571](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17201571/); [preprint PDF, U. Calgary](https://ucalgary.scholaris.ca/bitstreams/2376d7f2-d1c3-4ffe-ae50-4b9f7d88841a/download)
- Up to **20% of the adult population** regard themselves as chronic procrastinators in everyday life; among university students the figure is far higher, with **at least 50% reporting severe, consistent difficulty completing curricular work** (figures traced to Steel 2007 and restated in the subsequent treatment literature). — [Rozental et al., *Frontiers in Psychology* 2018, systematic review/meta-analysis of procrastination treatments](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2018.01588/full)
- Workplace: approximately **a quarter of the average working day** is characterizable as procrastination (D'Abate & Eddy, 2007), with an estimated loss of **$8,875 per employee per year** from personal/leisure activity during work hours. — [Metin, Taris & Peeters, "Measuring procrastination at work and its associated workplace aspects," *Personality and Individual Differences* (2016), citing D'Abate & Eddy 2007](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0191886916307474)
- The Procrastination at Work Scale (PAWS; Metin et al. 2016) splits workplace procrastination into two dimensions — **soldiering** (non-work-related slow-down without an alternative activity) and **cyberslacking**. Useful as a construct citation, not a prevalence citation. — [PAWS validation, *Frontiers in Psychology* 2021](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2021.726595/full)

### Inferences
- The strongest, most defensible hook statistic for a Plover case study is the Steel 2007 pair: ~20% of adults are chronic procrastinators; ~50% of university students procrastinate consistently and problematically. It is peer-reviewed, meta-analytic, in a top-tier journal, and the most cited work in the field.
- Workplace prevalence is materially less well-established than student prevalence. Any knowledge-worker procrastination claim should be framed as "a quarter of the working day" (D'Abate & Eddy) rather than as a headcount percentage.

### Gaps
- I could not verify the exact page/table in Steel (2007) for the 20%/50% figures directly — the hosted PDFs returned unparseable binary. The figures are consistently attributed to Steel 2007 across the peer-reviewed treatment literature (e.g., Rozental et al. 2018), but a report writer quoting them should cite Steel 2007 *via* a secondary peer-reviewed source, or verify page numbers against the published article.
- The widely repeated claim that **"88% of employees procrastinate for over 60 minutes daily"** surfaces mainly on content-marketing statistics aggregators (e.g., Zippia). **I found no primary study behind it. Do not use it.** — [Zippia procrastination statistics page (aggregator, weak sourcing)](https://www.zippia.com/advice/procrastination-statistics/)

## Why people procrastinate on long-horizon, ambiguous tasks specifically

### Takeaway
This is the best-evidenced part of the case, and it maps directly onto Plover's task-decomposition feature. Steel's meta-analysis identifies **task aversiveness, task delay (temporal distance of the payoff), low self-efficacy, and impulsiveness** as the strongest and most consistent predictors of procrastination. Temporal Motivation Theory formalizes this: motivation decays as reward recedes in time. And a 2022 experience-sampling study establishes the specific causal-direction finding Plover needs — **ambiguous tasks get procrastinated on more, within the same person**.

### Cited Findings
- Steel (2007) meta-analysis: "Strong and consistent predictors of procrastination were **task aversiveness, task delay, self-efficacy, and impulsiveness**, as well as conscientiousness and its facets of self-control, distractibility, organization, and achievement motivation." By contrast neuroticism, rebelliousness and sensation-seeking showed only weak connections. — [PubMed 17201571](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17201571/)
- Steel proposes **Temporal Motivation Theory** as the integrating account in the same paper: the motivational value of a task is its expectancy × value, divided by impulsiveness × delay. Mechanically, this means a task whose payoff is far in the future and whose probability of success feels uncertain has *low present motivational value* — the exact profile of a long-horizon intellectual project. — [Steel 2007, PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17201571/)
- Wieland, L. M., Hoppe, J. D., Wolgast, A., & Ebner-Priemer, U. W. (2022), "Task ambiguity and academic procrastination: An experience sampling approach," *Learning and Instruction* 81, Article 101595. Adaptive experience sampling, **N = 88 students, 6 prompts/day for 7 days, n = 3,581 measurements**, during exam preparation. — [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0959475222000160)
- Key result from that study: **30% of all *intended* study sessions were procrastinated**, and the risk that a session was procrastinated **increased with the student's task-related ambiguity perception**. This is a within-person, momentary finding, not a trait correlation — i.e. the same person procrastinates more on the tasks they perceive as more ambiguous. — [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0959475222000160)
- The theoretical lineage: Harris & Sutton (1983) proposed that task characteristics — **difficulty, appeal, ambiguity, and deadline pressure** — must be modeled alongside personal characteristics to explain procrastination. — cited in [Wieland et al. 2022](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0959475222000160) and in [Frontiers editorial, "New perspectives on procrastination, volume II" (2022)](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9382299/)
- A companion experience-sampling paper establishes within-person determinants of *delay onset* in goal-directed action generally. — [Wieland et al., "Predicting Delay in Goal-Directed Action," *Frontiers in Psychology* 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8356899/)

### Inferences
- Wieland et al. (2022) is the single most product-relevant citation available: "30% of intended study sessions were procrastinated, and ambiguity predicted which ones" is almost a literal statement of Plover's thesis — that the fix for delay is *making the next step concrete*.
- Steel's "task delay" predictor plus Temporal Motivation Theory gives the theoretical warrant for the *progress-visibility* half of Plover's pitch: shortening the perceived distance to a reward raises present motivational value. Decomposition into near-term subtasks with visible completion is, in TMT terms, a delay-reduction intervention.

### Gaps
- I could not retrieve the full text of Wieland et al. (2022) (ScienceDirect returned HTTP 403), so I do not have the effect size (odds ratio / coefficient) for the ambiguity→procrastination link, only its direction and significance. A report writer wanting an effect size must obtain the article.
- I found no study that directly measures "not knowing whether I'm making progress" as a distinct construct from task ambiguity. See the last section.

## Completion and attrition rates for long-horizon intellectual projects

### Takeaway
Roughly **43% of US PhD students do not finish within ten years** (Council of Graduate Schools, 2008), and the **median MOOC completion rate is 12.6%** across 221 courses (Jordan, 2015). Both are strong, well-sourced illustrations of long-horizon abandonment. UK figures are notably better (~73% within seven years), so the "PhD attrition" framing should specify the US.

### Cited Findings
- **US PhD non-completion: 43.4%.** The Council of Graduate Schools PhD Completion Project tracked ~49,000 students at 30 institutions across 54 disciplines and 330 programs; the **ten-year completion rate was 56.6%**. Primary report: Sowell, Zhang, Bell & Redd (2008), *PhD Completion and Attrition: Analysis of Baseline Demographic Data from the PhD Completion Project*. Data cover students entering AY1992-93 through AY2003-04. — [Council of Graduate Schools, PhD Completion and Attrition report (PDF)](https://cgsnet.org/wp-content/uploads/2022/01/phd_completion_and_attrition-2.pdf); [CGS project page](https://legacy.cgsnet.org/phd-completion-and-attrition-analysis-baseline-demographic-data-phd-completion-project-0); [PhD Completion Project](https://www.phdcompletion.org/)
- CGS follow-on: the *Doctoral Initiative on Minority Attrition and Completion* (2015) found the demographic groups driving enrollment growth (underrepresented minorities, women) had the lowest completion rates. — [CGS DIMAC report (PDF, 2015)](https://cgsnet.org/wp-content/uploads/2022/09/Doctoral_Initiative_on_Minority_Attrition_and_Completion-2015.pdf)
- **UK contrast: 72.9%** of UK/EU students starting full-time doctorates in 2010-11 were projected to obtain the degree within **seven years** (vs 70.1% for 2009-10 and 70.5% for 2008-09); **80.5%** within 25 years. Source: HEFCE estimation model built on institutions' annual HESA submissions, reported 2013. — [Times Higher Education, "PhD completion rates, 2013"](https://www.timeshighereducation.com/news/phd-completion-rates-2013/2006040.article); [HESA methodology note PITG 13/08](https://www.hesa.ac.uk/files/pisg/PITG_2013_02/PITG_13_08.pdf)
- **MOOC completion: median 12.6%**, range **0.7% to 52.1%**, across **221 MOOCs** from 78 institutions (Coursera, Open2Study, etc.). Jordan, K. (2015), "Massive Open Online Course Completion Rates Revisited: Assessment, Length and Attrition," *International Review of Research in Open and Distributed Learning* 16(3). — [IRRODL article](https://www.irrodl.org/index.php/irrodl/article/view/2112); [Jordan's dataset](http://www.katyjordan.com/MOOCproject.html)
- Jordan's mechanism finding is directly relevant to long-horizon abandonment: completion is **negatively correlated with course length**, higher for auto-graded assessment, and **the first and second weeks are the critical attrition window** — after which the proportion of active students stays remarkably stable. — [IRRODL article](https://www.irrodl.org/index.php/irrodl/article/view/2112)
- Earlier coverage establishing the ~5–10% MOOC completion norm in the popular record. — [Inside Higher Ed, "New study of low MOOC completion rates" (2013)](https://www.insidehighered.com/news/2013/05/10/new-study-low-mooc-completion-rates)

### Inferences
- "Nearly half of US PhD students never finish" (43.4% non-completion at ten years) is a credible, attributable hook statistic of the type the assignment asked for — an institutional dataset, named authors, named publisher, defined cohort.
- Jordan's "attrition happens in weeks 1–2, then stabilizes" is arguably more useful to Plover than the headline 12.6%: it says abandonment of long-horizon work is front-loaded, which is exactly when a decomposition + early-visible-progress intervention would bite.
- The US/UK PhD gap (56.6% at ten years vs ~73% at seven years) is large enough that any claim must specify the country and cohort. Do not blend them.

### Gaps
- **"All but dissertation" (ABD) prevalence:** I did not find a rigorous, current, primary-sourced figure for what share of doctoral non-completers leave specifically at the dissertation stage. The concept is well attested; the number is not. Do not quote an ABD percentage without a primary source.
- **Book-writing completion rates:** I found no credible quantified source. Figures circulating online (e.g. "97% of people who start a book never finish it") appear to be self-help folklore with no traceable study. **Recommend omitting entirely.**
- CGS figures are from 2008 (cohorts through 2003-04). I found no comparably scoped US replication published since. Date the figure explicitly when using it.

## To-do list / task manager abandonment and failure

### Takeaway
The famous statistic — **41% of to-do list items are never completed** — comes from an internal analysis by the app vendor iDoneThis (circa 2014), not from peer-reviewed research, and the original post is no longer readily retrievable. It should be cited as vendor data, hedged, and ideally not used as the lead statistic. I found no peer-reviewed equivalent.

### Cited Findings
- **41% of to-do list items are never completed**; ~50% are completed within a day, many within the first hour of being written down. Attributed to internal data from **iDoneThis**, a productivity/standup app. — [HuffPost, "Forty-One Percent of Tasks On To-Do Lists Are Never Done"](https://www.huffpost.com/entry/forty-one-percent-of-tasks-on-to-do-lists-are-never-done_b_9308978); [Forbes, Kevin Kruse, "Your To-Do List: Where Important Things Go To Die" (2016)](https://www.forbes.com/sites/kevinkruse/2016/02/17/your-to-do-list-where-important-things-go-to-die/); [CUInsight](https://www.cuinsight.com/41-of-items-on-the-average-to-do-list-are-never-finished/)
- Companion iDoneThis claim: **only ~15% of tasks completed during a day started out as to-do list items** (i.e. the large majority of completed work was unplanned). Same sourcing caveats. — [NBC News, "Why Your 'To Do' List Is Failing You"](https://www.nbcnews.com/id/wbna55607471); [Fast Company, "Why Creating A To-Do List Is Derailing Your Success"](https://www.fastcompany.com/3056852/why-creating-a-to-do-list-is-derailing-your-success)
- The stated *reason* given in secondary coverage for the 41% — that uncompleted items tend to be **too vague, unprioritized, or never reviewed** — is commentary, not a measured finding. Treat as interpretation. — [iDoneThis blog](https://blog.idonethis.com/unplanned-tasks/)

### Inferences
- This is the weakest evidence category in the brief. It is the statistic most likely to be challenged in a case study, because it is vendor-generated, methodologically undescribed, and the originating blog post has largely vanished from the web while the number keeps circulating.
- Recommended handling: use it as *color* ("a widely cited figure from the task app iDoneThis puts it at 41%, though the underlying methodology was never published"), never as the hook. The Wieland et al. 30%-of-intended-sessions-procrastinated figure is a stronger, peer-reviewed substitute that makes essentially the same point.

### Gaps
- **No peer-reviewed study on to-do list item completion rates was found.** Academic work on personal task management (e.g. Bellotti, Whittaker) is qualitative/HCI and does not yield a completion-rate percentage.
- No Todoist-published completion-rate figure was located in this pass. Todoist has historically published aggregate productivity data; a targeted follow-up on Todoist's own research posts may yield a better-documented vendor number.

## The experience of not knowing whether you're making progress: feedback loops in cognitive work

### Takeaway
There is no single study measuring "I can't tell if I'm making progress" as a construct, but two strong literatures converge on it. **Feedback** is one of the five core dimensions of the Hackman & Oldham Job Characteristics Model (1976) — the dimension that supplies "knowledge of results," and it is structurally weakest in open-ended cognitive work. And Amabile & Kramer's diary research provides the positive-case statistic Plover actually wants: **progress was present on 76% of people's best-mood days.**

### Cited Findings
- **Amabile & Kramer's progress principle**: an analysis of nearly **12,000 daily diary entries from 238 employees across 7 companies**, published as "The Power of Small Wins," *Harvard Business Review*, May 2011, and the book *The Progress Principle* (2011). — [HBR, "The Power of Small Wins"](https://hbr.org/2011/05/the-power-of-small-wins); [full text PDF](https://www.develor.si/wp-content/uploads/2022/05/The-power-of-small-wins.pdf)
- Headline finding: **"of all the things that can boost inner work life, the most important is making progress in meaningful work"** — steps forward occurred on **76% of people's best-mood days**, making progress the most common trigger of a "best day." — [HBR 2011](https://hbr.org/2011/05/the-power-of-small-wins); [Harvard Business School Working Knowledge](https://www.library.hbs.edu/working-knowledge/how-small-wins-unleash-creativity)
- **Small** wins count: **28% of small events had a major effect on inner work life** — incremental forward movement matters far more to daily engagement and creativity than managers assume. — [HBR 2011](https://hbr.org/2011/05/the-power-of-small-wins); [Creativity at Work summary](https://www.creativityatwork.com/on-the-power-of-small-wins/)
- **Job Characteristics Model** (Hackman & Oldham, 1976, *Organizational Behavior and Human Performance*): five core dimensions — skill variety, task identity, task significance, autonomy, and **feedback**. Feedback produces the critical psychological state "knowledge of the actual results of the work activities." — [Hackman & Oldham (1976) original paper, PDF](http://www.iot.ntnu.no/innovation/norsi-pims-courses/huber/Hackman%20&%20Oldham%20(1976).pdf)
- Meta-analytic validation: Fried, Y. & Ferris, G. R. (1987), "The Validity of the Job Characteristics Model: A Review and Meta-Analysis," *Personnel Psychology* 40(2) — reviews nearly 200 studies / meta-analyzes ~76, finding job characteristics significantly related to psychological and behavioral outcomes, though the mediating-psychological-states pattern was less well supported. — [Wiley](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/j.1744-6570.1987.tb00605.x)
- Recent re-validation of feedback as a work-engagement driver alongside autonomy, skill variety, task identity and task significance. — [Cotič et al., *Global Business and Organizational Excellence* (2025)](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/joe.22295)

### Inferences
- The Amabile 76% figure is the best *inverse* framing available for Plover: if progress drives the best days, then work that hides its own progress systematically removes the strongest available motivator. That is a defensible, HBR-attributable, 12,000-diary-entry-backed claim.
- The JCM "feedback" dimension gives the structural argument its academic footing: manual and transactional work carries intrinsic feedback (the widget is made, the ticket is closed); open-ended cognitive work on a multi-month deliverable does not, so the feedback dimension is near-zero by default and must be engineered in. This is framing supported by the model, not a measured finding — state it as an argument, not a statistic.

### Gaps
- I found **no study that directly quantifies the absence of progress feedback in knowledge work versus manual work.** The manual-vs-cognitive feedback contrast is an inference from the JCM, not an empirical comparison I could source. Do not present it as a finding.
- The claimed meta-analytic correlation of **r = 0.825 between job feedback and job satisfaction** that appeared in search results is implausibly high for this literature and its provenance was unclear (it appears to come from a single-study secondary source, not Fried & Ferris). **Do not use that number** without verification against Fried & Ferris (1987) directly.
- Peter Drucker's knowledge-worker productivity writing ("Knowledge-Worker Productivity: The Biggest Challenge," *California Management Review*, 1999) is available as framing — Drucker's point that knowledge work requires the worker to define *what the task is* dovetails with the task-ambiguity literature — but I did not retrieve it in this pass and it contains no statistics in any case.

## Knowledge worker productivity perception and time fragmentation

### Takeaway
Industry survey data (flagged as vendor research, not peer-reviewed) shows knowledge workers spend the majority of their day on coordination overhead rather than the skilled work they were hired for, across a heavily fragmented day. Asana's Anatomy of Work Index is the most-cited instance.

### Cited Findings
- Asana **Anatomy of Work Index** surveyed **10,223 knowledge workers** across multiple countries (knowledge worker defined as spending ≥50% of time using a device to complete tasks). **Vendor research — flag as such.** — [Asana, Anatomy of Work Index](https://asana.com/resources/anatomy-of-work-index)
- Findings: the average worker spends about **a quarter of their time on skills-based work** (coding, designing, creating) and **13% on strategic planning**, with roughly **60% of the day** on "work about work" — communicating about tasks, hunting for documents, and managing shifting priorities. — [Asana, "How Work About Work Gets in the Way of Real Work"](https://asana.com/resources/why-work-about-work-is-bad)
- Annualized: **103 hours in unnecessary meetings, 209 hours of duplicative work, and 352 hours talking about work rather than doing it**, per worker per year. — [Asana](https://asana.com/resources/why-work-about-work-is-bad)
- Fragmentation: workers toggle between an average of **10 different apps, 25 times per day**. — [Asana, "The Way We Work Isn't Working"](https://asana.com/resources/work-isnt-working)

### Inferences
- These figures support the "your day disappears and you cannot point to what moved" intuition, but they measure *time allocation*, not *perceived progress*. They are adjacent evidence, not direct evidence, and they come from a company that sells work-management software — a conflict worth disclosing in a case study that is itself pitching a productivity product.

### Gaps
- I found **no credible survey asking knowledge workers directly whether they feel they accomplished anything** — the most on-point possible statistic for Plover. Several aggregator sites (speakwiseapp.com, myhours.com, clickup.com) surface such claims but are SEO statistics roundups without traceable primary sources; I excluded them all.
- Asana's index figures come from different survey waves (2021 and later); the specific wave for each number should be confirmed on Asana's page before quoting, since Asana re-dates its resource pages ("[2026]") without necessarily re-fielding the survey.

## Recommended hook statistics, ranked by defensibility

1. **43.4% of US PhD students do not complete within ten years** (Council of Graduate Schools PhD Completion Project, ~49,000 students, published 2008) — institutional dataset, named authors, the strongest "long-horizon work gets abandoned" number available.
2. **~20% of adults are chronic procrastinators; ~50% of university students procrastinate consistently and problematically** (Steel, *Psychological Bulletin*, 2007, 691 correlations) — the field-standard peer-reviewed prevalence anchor.
3. **30% of intended study sessions were procrastinated, and ambiguity predicted which ones** (Wieland et al., *Learning and Instruction*, 2022, N=88, 3,581 measurements) — the closest thing to a direct empirical statement of Plover's decomposition thesis.
4. **Progress occurred on 76% of people's best-mood days** (Amabile & Kramer, HBR 2011, ~12,000 diary entries) — the motivational payoff Plover's progress visibility is trying to restore.
5. **Median MOOC completion 12.6%, with attrition front-loaded into weeks 1–2** (Jordan, IRRODL 2015, 221 courses) — vivid illustration of long-horizon abandonment.

Use with caution or not at all: the 41% to-do-list figure (vendor, unpublished methodology), the 88%-of-employees figure (no primary source found), any ABD percentage (no primary source found), any book-completion percentage (folklore), and r = 0.825 for feedback→satisfaction (implausible, unverified).
