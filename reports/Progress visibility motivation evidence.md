# Progress visibility works — but measurement is the unsolved half

Plover's core thesis survives contact with the evidence, with one important amendment. The strongest citation available — Harkin et al.'s 2016 *Psychological Bulletin* meta-analysis of **138 randomized studies and 19,951 participants** — finds that monitoring goal progress raises goal attainment at **d+ = 0.40 [95% CI 0.32, 0.48]**, and that the effect is **larger when progress is publicly reported and when it is physically recorded** ([PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26479070/)). That moderator is the single most directly quotable sentence in the literature for a product whose entire premise is rendering progress as a visible artifact. Critically, the effect is domain-general, not fitness-specific: self-monitoring lifts academic performance at **Hedges' g = 0.47** across 36 studies and 2,617 students ([Learning and Individual Differences, 2022](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0883035522000179)), bracketing the fitness-tracker effect range of roughly 0.3–0.6. So the right framing is not "a fitness mechanism transplanted to knowledge work" but "fitness trackers are the proven consumer-grade implementation of a domain-general mechanism that already works in cognitive settings." What the literature does *not* supply is the thing Plover actually has to build: no study exists on writing or professional knowledge-worker output tracking, and no study compares passively-sensed against self-reported progress on the same cognitive goal. Meanwhile the competitive read is sharp — no shipping product infers task progress from activity sensing, but decomposition, local-first architecture, screen capture and always-on-top overlays are all commodities, and the best-funded local screen-watching Mac product pivoted away from the category entirely.

And on the workplace side, roughly **a quarter of the average working day** is characterizable as procrastination (D'Abate & Eddy, 2007, restated in [Metin, Taris & Peeters, 2016](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0191886916307474)) — use that rather than any headcount percentage, because workplace prevalence is far less well-established than student prevalence.

### The 10,000-step story is the best rhetorical asset in the file

The number originates from a **1965 Japanese marketing campaign** by Yamasa, a clock company, for the *manpo-kei* (万歩計), literally "10,000-step meter," launched into the fitness enthusiasm that followed the 1964 Tokyo Olympics ([Wikipedia: Pedometer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pedometer)). Harvard epidemiologist I-Min Lee is the standard attributed debunker: no studies examined 10,000 steps when the device was developed — "It was a made-up number in the sense that 10,000 sounds good, it's easy to remember" ([Popular Science](https://www.popsci.com/story/health/10000-steps-evidence-study/)). Sixty years later, the best dose-response evidence puts the meaningful threshold at **7,000 steps/day**, associated versus 2,000 steps with **47% lower all-cause mortality, 38% lower dementia risk, and 22% lower risk of depressive symptoms**, from 31 meta-analyzed studies across 34 cohorts ([Ding et al., 2025, *The Lancet Public Health*](https://www.thelancet.com/journals/lanpub/article/PIIS2468-2667(25)00164-1/fulltext); [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40713949/)). A copywriter's round number, wrong by 30%, moved hundreds of millions of people for half a century. The *visible target* did the work; the *number* was interchangeable. That reframes the obvious objection to Plover — "you can't measure cognitive output precisely" — as less damning than it looks, since step counting is also a poor health proxy (it ignores intensity and resistance training entirely) and worked anyway. The inverse risk is worth naming too: 10,000 was too *high* relative to evidence, and unattainable targets plausibly feed the demotivation-driven abandonment documented below.

### What glanceability implies about the UI

The most transferable HCI finding in the file: **over 70% of physical-activity-tracker usage consists of glances — brief ~5-second sessions where the user checks activity levels with no further interaction** ([Gouveia et al., UbiComp 2016](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/2971648.2971754)). The paper derives six design qualities, one of which — **supporting comparisons to targets and norms** — is the formal statement of why an arbitrary target still functions: the glance needs a referent to be legible. No target, no glance value. This reframes the tracker as an ambient status display answering "am I on track?" in under a second, rather than a dashboard, and it is direct warrant for an overlay HUD over a full analytics view.

## Do not cite these — a consolidated blacklist

This is the section worth keeping open while drafting. Each of these circulates widely enough that a reviewer might expect to see it; each would damage the case study's credibility.

**Ariely & Wertenbroch (2002), "Procrastination, Deadlines, and Performance."** The canonical citation for "evenly spaced intermediate deadlines beat one final deadline" **was retracted from *Psychological Science* on 2 September 2026** for data tampering ([retraction notice](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/full/10.1177/09567976261488042); [Retraction Watch](https://retractionwatch.com/2026/09/03/procrastination-study-duke-dan-ariely-psychological-science-data-colada-tampering-retraction/)). Coauthor Wertenbroch himself requested the retraction on 23 July 2026, citing a failed replication and Data Colada analyses that "called into question the veracity of the overall findings." The paper had roughly 1,000 citations. The replication — Hyndman & Bisin (2026) — found **evenly spaced deadlines had a negligible effect on performance** in adults ([SAGE](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/09567976261460772)). Any decomposition argument should route through implementation intentions (d = 0.65) instead, which is a stronger effect from an intact literature.

**"41% of to-do list items are never completed."** Internal analysis by the productivity vendor **iDoneThis**, circa 2014; never peer-reviewed, methodology never published, and the originating post has largely disappeared while the number keeps circulating ([HuffPost](https://www.huffpost.com/entry/forty-one-percent-of-tasks-on-to-do-lists-are-never-done_b_9308978)). The companion claim that only ~15% of tasks completed in a day started as to-do items carries the same defect. No peer-reviewed study of to-do-list completion rates exists. Substitute Wieland et al.'s **30% of intended study sessions procrastinated**, which makes the same point from a peer-reviewed within-person design.

**"88% of employees procrastinate for over 60 minutes daily."** Surfaces only on content-marketing statistics aggregators such as Zippia. **No primary study was found behind it.**

**Unattributed permission-lift percentages.** The circulating figures — "compelling explanations produce up to an **81% increase** in granted requests," "**82% of users want a clear reason**" (loosely attributed to Nielsen Norman Group with no working citation), "contextually relevant requests see up to a **28% higher grant rate**," "pre-permission screens increase IDFA opt-in by **20–40 percentage points**," "**43%** uninstall apps requiring too many permissions," "**72%** uninstall due to privacy concerns" — are all vendor or commentary numbers with no traceable methodology ([Dogtown Media](https://www.dogtownmedia.com/the-ask-when-and-how-to-request-mobile-app-permissions-camera-location-contacts/); [Playwire](https://www.playwire.com/blog/mastering-idfa-opt-in-rates-the-complete-apptrackingtransparency-guide-for-ios-apps)). The 81% NN/g figure could not be traced to any actual NN/g publication. **The direction of the effect is platform-endorsed and near-universally practiced; the magnitude is not reliably quantified in any transparent public source.**

**Endeavour Partners' "one-third abandon within six months."** A **2014 consultancy white paper**, n≈1,700 US consumers, never peer-reviewed, describing 2014 hardware and app ecosystems ([Inside Wearables Part 2](https://medium.com/@endeavourprtnrs/inside-wearables-part-2-july-2014-ef301d425cdd)). Worse, the same firm's earlier 2013 round reported **about half** of consumers abandoning their trackers ([Part 1](https://medium.com/@endeavourprtnrs/inside-wearable-how-the-science-of-human-behavior-change-offers-the-secret-to-long-term-engagement-a15b3c7d4cf3)) — the widely-repeated "one-third" is the more favorable of two numbers from the same source. Cite it, if at all, as *the origin of a widely-repeated claim*, and pair it with peer-reviewed data that is considerably harsher: a prospective cohort found initial engagement of **73% collapsing to 14% at month 5 and 5% at month 7** ([*JMIR mHealth and uHealth*, doi:10.2196/80465](https://doi.org/10.2196/80465)).

**Four smaller traps.** A widely-circulated 2025 piece claims trackers make users "**seven times more likely** to stick to workouts" ([The Conversation](https://theconversation.com/wearable-fitness-trackers-can-make-you-seven-times-more-likely-to-stick-to-your-workouts-new-research-256941)) — a 7x odds ratio sits far outside every meta-analytic effect range above and is almost certainly an odds ratio on a narrow adherence outcome; do not cite without reading the underlying paper. A claimed **r = 0.825 correlation between job feedback and job satisfaction** is implausibly high for that literature and does not come from Fried & Ferris (1987); drop it. **ABD (all-but-dissertation) prevalence** figures have no rigorous primary source. And **book-completion statistics** ("97% of people who start a book never finish it") are self-help folklore with no traceable study — omit entirely.

**One more to handle carefully rather than ban.** Amabile & Kramer's percentages are the weakest sourcing among the findings you will most want to use. The methodology is solid and consistently reported — approximately **12,000 daily diary entries from 238 professionals across 7 companies** — and the thesis is primary-verified: of everything that can boost emotions, motivation and perceptions during a workday, **the single most important is making progress in meaningful work** ([HBR, May 2011](https://hbr.org/2011/05/the-power-of-small-wins)). But the specific numbers — **progress present on 76% of best-mood days versus setbacks on only 13%**, **28% of minor events having a major effect on inner work life**, and **only 5% of 669 surveyed managers ranking "supporting progress" as the top motivator** (they ranked it last of five) — were only obtainable from secondary summaries this pass because HBR's page is paywalled ([PDF copy](https://www.develor.si/wp-content/uploads/2022/05/The-power-of-small-wins.pdf); [Creativity at Work](https://www.creativityatwork.com/on-the-power-of-small-wins/)). Verify against the HBR full text or the book before printing. Also note the epistemic class: this is descriptive diary frequency analysis, not an experiment. It cannot bear causal weight, and no effect sizes or inferential statistics exist for it.

## The counter-evidence a credible case study has to name

**Progress framing reverses over a goal's life.** Koo & Fishbach's small-area hypothesis holds that people "exhibit greater motivation when their attention is directed to whichever is **smaller in size** — their accumulated or remaining progress" ([*Journal of Consumer Research* 39(3), 493–509](https://academic.oup.com/jcr/article-abstract/39/3/493/1822606)). Early in pursuit, showing accumulated progress wins ("20% completed" beats "80% remaining"); late in pursuit it flips and remaining progress is the stronger motivator. The underlying commitment account is that **to-date information signals commitment and helps when commitment is uncertain, while to-go information signals shortfall and helps when commitment is already certain** ([Koo & Fishbach, 2008, *JPSP* 94(2), 183–195](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18211171/)). The direct implication for Plover: a static progress display that always shows the same framing is *provably* suboptimal for part of the goal's life.

**Perceived progress licenses disengagement.** Fishbach & Dhar's four studies demonstrate that "progress along one goal liberates people to pursue inconsistent goals" — making progress toward a goal can produce disengagement *from that goal*, and merely *planning* future progress can license incongruent immediate action ([*Journal of Consumer Research* 32(3), 370–377](https://academic.oup.com/jcr/article-abstract/32/3/370/1867208)). Taken with the small-area result, the same "60% done" readout that motivates an uncommitted beginner can license a committed user to coast. Be honest about the limits here too: no effect sizes were retrievable (both JCR full texts returned 403), no meta-analysis of the licensing effect was located, and adjacent literatures such as ego depletion and moral licensing have had notable replication problems. Licensing is well-published, not settled.

The honest version of Plover's claim is therefore narrower than "progress visibility increases motivation." It is: **well-framed, recorded, externally-visible progress increases goal attainment by a reliable medium-sized margin, and the correct framing changes over the life of the goal.**

**Stated privacy concern does not predict behavior.** A meta-analysis of **181 independent studies (N = 99,092)** found privacy concern "exhibited a larger impact on behavioral intentions than on actual behavior" — the privacy paradox generalizes across the literature ([*Technological Forecasting and Social Change*, 2023](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0040162523004742)), corroborated by [Kokolakis (2017)](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0167404815001017) and [Barth & de Jong (2017)](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0736585317302022). Privacy guarantees are unlikely to be a conversion driver on their own. The defensible role is narrower and actually more interesting: **privacy work pays off at the gate, not in the funnel.** The permission dialog is the one situation where the privacy decision and the behavior are the *same action*, which is exactly where the paradox is weakest. Pew's finding that **73% feel they have little or no control** over company data collection also means Plover's window-scoping control targets the most widely reported deficit, which is control rather than concern itself.

**Just-in-time requests beat front-loaded ones — which challenges Plover's onboarding.** The Berkeley line of work established that install-time permission manifests are largely unattended and uncomprehended ([Felt et al., SOUPS 2012](https://cups.cs.cmu.edu/soups/2012/proceedings/a3_Felt.pdf)), and that privacy decisions are fundamentally **contextual**: a user asked mid-task "may have some understanding of why the application needs that permission based on what she was doing" ([Wijesekera et al., USENIX Security 2015](https://www.usenix.org/system/files/conference/usenixsecurity15/sec15-paper-wijesekera.pdf)). A 131-person longitudinal field study built a contextual classifier predicting users' own privacy decisions at **96.8% accuracy, a four-fold error reduction versus ask-on-first-use** ([IEEE S&P 2017](https://arxiv.org/abs/1703.02090)). The reported field figures — **95% of participants blocked at least one permission request, with an average denial rate of 60%** — came from search extraction of PDFs that could not be parsed in this pass and should be verified before printing. The uncomfortable inference: Plover front-loads all three guarantees during onboarding, before the user has experienced any need for progress inference, which sits closer to *install-time* than to *just-in-time* in this taxonomy — the very pattern the research found ineffective. Vendor data complicates rather than rescues this: AppsFlyer found **30% of non-gaming apps got higher opt-in at day 7 than at first launch**, but the **highest rates overall still came from prompting at first launch** ([AppsFlyer](https://www.appsflyer.com/blog/trends-insights/att-opt-in-rates-higher/)).

Two arguments genuinely survive in Plover's favor here, and neither requires a grant-rate statistic. The first is mechanical: on macOS, a denied screen-recording permission requires a System Settings trip plus an app relaunch to reverse, so an uninformed denial is close to permanent. **A pre-permission screen converts an irreversible one-shot into a reversible conversation.** The second is comprehension-based: a CHI 2025 study found employees were **confident in their knowledge of workplace-monitoring terms but unable to accurately define them**, with awareness "generally low and lacking certainty" ([ACM](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3706598.3713651)). If users are confidently wrong about what monitoring software does, a settings page they never open cannot correct it; an explicit guarantee screen at the moment of the ask can. Front-loading is a comprehension intervention, not merely a reassurance one.

**The baseline suspicion Plover inherits is well-quantified.** APA's 2023 Work in America survey (Harris Poll, 2,515 employed US adults, ±3.1pp) found **51% of workers aware their employer monitors them**; among monitored workers, **47% worry about being spied on versus 22% of unmonitored**, **46% feel uncomfortable versus 23%**, and **45% say their workplace negatively affects their mental health versus 29%** ([APA](https://www.apa.org/pubs/reports/work-in-america/2023-work-america-ai-monitoring)). **36% of monitored employees believe they do not matter to their employer, versus 22% of unmonitored** ([APA](https://www.apa.org/topics/healthy-workplaces/employee-electronic-monitoring)). Because roughly half of US workers already live with employer monitoring, a self-installed screen-reading tool is evaluated against a salient negative prior, and "no manager view" carries more weight than the other guarantees. Note what local storage does *not* answer: the bossware literature's measurement paradox holds that people optimize for the sensor and that surveillance degrades the work it measures. Local-only architecture addresses *who sees it*, not *what being watched does to me*.

**What is missing on the privacy side.** No survey ranks screen-recording permission against camera, microphone or location on user comfort — a claim that screen recording is uniquely sensitive is a plausible but unevidenced design judgment. No macOS TCC grant-rate benchmark exists publicly. No study measures whether "on-device" messaging increases willingness to grant a permission. No peer-reviewed randomized experiment on rationale-screen grant-rate effects was located, and no public onboarding A/B dataset with an explainer arm exists. And no research covers how consumers evaluate *self-installed* monitoring where the user is also the beneficiary — the exact distinction Plover depends on is untested. Also worth flagging: one recent preprint argues directly that "local is not a sufficient privacy boundary," since moving processing on-device "changes the relevant adversary rather than removing it" ([arXiv 2606.10173](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2606.10173)).

## Where Plover's differentiation genuinely holds, and where it collapses

The competitive picture is cleaner than most, because the category boundary is sharp. Every major automatic tracker captures the same primitive — foreground app plus window title, plus URL and idle state, sampled continuously — and differs only in whether it categorizes, bills, or drafts a timesheet from that stream. **The universal output unit is elapsed time attributed to a bucket, not completion against a defined deliverable.** Memtime captures app activity and window titles with no screenshots and no server-side processing ([Memtime](https://www.memtime.com/)). Rize markets "monitor project progress," but the described mechanism is **keyword matching against the activity timeline** to split and assign time entries — attribution of hours to a project, an input measure, not fraction-complete on a deliverable ([Rize](https://rize.io/features/automatic-time-tracking)). Timely's background "Memory" tracker feeds an AI layer that produces a **timesheet draft** for billing ([Timely](https://www.timely.com/timely-features/)). RescueTime sets goals on *time spent*, with its "Focus Work" category defined by how the user manually scored activities ([RescueTime](https://help.rescuetime.com/article/44-how-to-set-goals)).

The single most citable boundary in the whole landscape is RescueTime's Focus Sessions. The user optionally types a task description at session start, distractions are blocked, and at the end **"the Assistant asks whether you completed your work"** ([RescueTime](https://help.rescuetime.com/article/297-how-to-start-a-focus-session)). RescueTime has both the activity stream and a named task, has had them for over a decade, and still defers to a human yes/no. That is strong evidence the inference step is genuinely hard rather than merely un-attempted — and it lets the case study frame Plover's bet as attacking precisely the step incumbents route around.

The other half of the honest assessment is less flattering. **Goal decomposition is commodity.** Goblin Tools' Magic ToDo has done it free on the web since roughly 2023 — enter a task, set a "spiciness" level, get a breakdown with more steps for spicier tasks, exportable to Todoist, Asana, Notion, TickTick and others ([goblin.tools](https://goblin.tools/ToDo); [GIGAZINE](https://gigazine.net/gsc_news/en/20250326-magic-todo)) — and at least one goal-first AI planner, Skedul.AI, markets goal → milestones → daily actions → schedule ([skedul.ai](https://skedul.ai/blog/sunsama-vs-motion-vs-reclaim)). Decomposition is table stakes Plover needs in order to have a *denominator* for the progress bar, not a differentiator. Working-hours-aware scheduling is a crowded, well-funded market (Motion, Reclaim, Sunsama, Akiflow). **Continuous local screen capture is a solved open-source commodity** — Screenpipe captures screen and audio 100% locally across Windows, macOS and Linux with plugin-based AI analysis ([Screenpipe](https://screenpipe.com/blog/best-rewind-ai-alternative-2026)) — so the sensing layer is not defensible either. Local-first is likewise table stakes rather than a moat, already owned in the adjacent tracking category by Memtime and Screenpipe, and Plover's own architecture qualifies the claim: goal text is sent to Gemini through a backend proxy. The honest phrasing is "**activity data never leaves the device; goal text is sent to an LLM**," and stating it plainly is better than having a technical reader catch it. Finally, **always-on-top overlay windows are trivially available** and used by dozens of utilities — WindowTop, Hover, SnapDraw ([Hover on the App Store](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/hover-always-on-top-windows/id1502873830?mt=12)). The claim that holds is "no one has put goal progress in a persistent HUD"; the claim that does not is "persistent overlays are novel."

So the white space is real but narrow and specific: **the inference layer, the function `(window content, task definition) → fraction complete`.** Both neighboring layers are commoditized, and a competitor could bolt the same layer onto Screenpipe in a weekend. There is at least one credible artifact of unmet demand — AI founder Matt Shumer, September 2025: "I would pay for a to-do list app that watches my screen and automatically checks off items/moves them to in-flight as I work" ([X](https://x.com/mattshumer_/status/1964504207316439107)). The nearest research precedent infers user *actions*, not goal progress ([*The Invisible Mentor*, arXiv 2509.26557](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.26557v1)).

**Two hard questions the case study should not dodge.** First, **Rewind AI — the 2022 Mac app doing continuous screen recording plus AI search — rebranded to Limitless in 2024 and pivoted from a Mac app to a cloud-connected hardware pendant** ([Screenpipe](https://screenpipe.com/blog/best-rewind-ai-alternative-2026), competitor-authored so the editorializing is partisan though the pivot is well-attested; [early-adopter account](https://andrewschreiber.substack.com/p/an-early-adopters-thoughts-on-rewindais)). The best-funded attempt at "an app that watches your screen all day for your own benefit" moved *away* from local desktop screen capture. The available answer — that Rewind sold *recall*, a search problem with weak daily pull, while Plover sells *momentum*, a motivation problem with a daily hook — is a defensible distinction but a claim, not a proven one. Second, the fitness-tracker analogy is rhetorically strong and technically weak: a step counter has physical, unambiguous ground truth; "how far through drafting this memo am I" has none. YC company HYBRD executes the same aggregate-sensor-data-and-adapt-the-plan pattern in fitness, where the sensor problem is easy ([YC Health Tech](https://www.ycombinator.com/companies/health-tech)) — underlining that the analogy's power comes from sensor reliability Plover does not inherit.

**Respect the verification boundary.** Several products in the landscape were **not primary-verified** and must not be stated as fact: Toggl Track, ActivityWatch, Timing (macOS), Akiflow, Amie, and Todoist's current AI features all failed to return primary sources. Motion's lack of subtasks came only from third-party comparison sites and should be spot-checked on usemotion.com. Timely's 2026 sub-task sync from Asana and ClickUp came from a third-party review site. Apple Live Activities — probably the strongest legitimizing precedent available for a persistent-progress HUD pattern — needs a citation to apple.com before use. Most importantly, the white-space finding rests on roughly eleven searches with shallow YC-directory and Product Hunt coverage: **the case study should say "we found none," never "there are none."**

## The gaps that decide how confident the case study can sound

Four holes matter enough to name explicitly, because naming them is what separates a credible portfolio piece from a pitch deck.

**No study exists on writing or professional knowledge-worker output tracking.** The self-monitoring literature covers students, not working professionals. The g = 0.47 academic effect is the closest available proxy and it is a proxy.

**No study compares passively-sensed against self-reported progress on the same cognitive goal.** This is precisely Plover's technical crux. Steps are automatic and passively sensed; study and writing progress are typically self-reported, which reintroduces the exact effort cost that glanceable trackers eliminated. Nothing in the literature establishes whether the fitness-tracker advantage survives the transplant, and no source found solves it. Note also that no evidence *against* transfer was found either — which may reflect publication bias rather than genuine absence of null results.

**No published accuracy figures exist for LLM-based progress estimation on knowledge work**, so nothing validates or refutes whether the core inference is feasible at a quality users would tolerate. Relatedly, **no source addresses whether users want a progress bar that might be wrong** — a mis-estimating bar may be worse than none.

**No software-specific experimental evidence for progress bars.** The design-community claims trace overwhelmingly back to consumer/loyalty-card studies — Kivetz, Urminsky & Zheng's goal-gradient work ([*JMR* 43(1), 39–58](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1509/jmkr.43.1.39)) and Nunes & Drèze's endowed-progress car-wash study ([SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=991962)), which are frequently and incorrectly conflated. Both establish something stronger than "progress motivates" — that **perceived** remaining distance, not actual remaining work, governs effort — but the famous numbers (café median completion of 10 versus 15 days; the car wash's 34% versus 19% completion, roughly a 1.8x lift from framing alone) surfaced only in secondary sources this pass and should be verified at source before quoting. A case study is on safe ground saying progress-bar design *draws on* endowed-progress and goal-gradient findings, and on shaky ground implying a body of software-specific experimental proof. Similarly, ring-closing and streaks are credited mechanisms, not measured ones: **no RCT isolates ring-closing versus numeric display, or streak-present versus streak-absent**, and the available streak material is vendor content marketing.

Finally, two facts on abandonment worth carrying into any retention argument. Attig & Franke's review plus survey of 159 former tracker users found that **permanent abandonment decisions related specifically to loss of tracking motivation**, whereas abandonment attributed to inaccuracy or uselessness correlated with general negative attitudes toward personal quantification ([*Computers in Human Behavior* 102, 2020](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0747563219303127)). Fixable UX complaints produce temporary lapses; motivational exhaustion produces permanent exit. And wearable adherence among insufficiently-active young adults was found **independent of identity and motivation for physical activity**, with wear time *negatively* associated with integrated regulation ([*Journal of Behavioral Medicine*, 2023](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10865-023-00444-4)) — the dropoff is a property of the artifact, not of the user.

## Conclusion

The evidence supports a more specific and more interesting thesis than the one Plover probably started with. The active ingredient is not pedometry and not gamification — it is **externally recorded, publicly legible progress feedback**, a domain-general mechanism with effect sizes clustering at d ≈ 0.4–0.6 whether the goal is walking, studying, or anything else Harkin's 138 studies covered. Fitness trackers are not the source of the mechanism; they are the proof that a consumer-grade implementation of it can reach population scale, doubling US ownership from **21% in 2020** ([Pew](https://www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2020/01/09/about-one-in-five-americans-use-a-smart-watch-or-fitness-tracker/)) to roughly **43–46% by 2025** ([Rock Health via Fierce Healthcare](https://www.fiercehealthcare.com/health-tech/health-wearable-ownership-33-past-decade-rock-health-survey)) — while still hemorrhaging existing users to abandonment. That combination, saturation plus attrition, is exactly the condition under which retention mechanics rather than acquisition become the competitive ground.

Which puts the burden in an unusual place. The motivational science is the *settled* part of Plover's argument; the unsettled part is measurement. Every finding that makes the progress bar compelling — glanceability, endowed progress, the small-area reversal, the 10,000-step lesson that an imperfect visible target beats an invisible accurate one — presupposes a number to display. Fitness solved that with a physical sensor and an arbitrary threshold nobody questioned for fifty years. Plover has to solve it with an LLM reading window content, against a goal whose ground truth does not exist, in a category where the incumbent with the longest-running sensor data still asks the human "did you finish?" The strongest form of the case study's argument is therefore not that progress visibility motivates — that is established and mildly boring — but that **the inference layer is the one genuinely unbuilt piece in an otherwise commoditized stack**, and that the 10,000-step precedent suggests the bar for "good enough to motivate" is considerably lower than the bar for "accurate."
